How are computers able to simulate and render fluids? With so many molecules and extremely complex-looking patterns, how can a computer with digital logic even attempt this task?

A fluid is made up of an enormous number of molecules interacting at the microscopic scale. Tracking the behavior of every molecule is effectively impossible, but we can describe the behavior of a fluid as a whole using mathematical equations.

The first step is to let go of the idea of discrete molecules and instead think of the fluid as a continuous substance that fills space. By treating the fluid as a continuum, we can use vector and scalar fields to describe its properties at every point.

As with most physical systems, these fields are governed by differential equations that describe how they change over time and space. The fundamental equations that govern fluid dynamics are known as the Navier-Stokes equations.

# The Navier-Stokes Equations

We start with Newton's second law:

$$
\mathbf{F} = m \mathbf{a}
$$

<Info>
  Bold terms are vectors. In this case, force and acceleration both have a
  direction.
</Info>

Since we are dealing with fluids, we can express the mass ($m$) as density ($\rho$) times volume ($V$), and acceleration ($\mathbf{a}$) as the material derivative of velocity ($\frac{D\mathbf{u}}{Dt}$):

$$
\mathbf{F} = \rho V \frac{D\mathbf{u}}{Dt}
$$

## The Material Derivative

Imagine a tiny packet moving through a velocity field. It can accelerate because the velocity at its current position changes over time, but it can also accelerate by moving into a region where the velocity is different. The material derivative accounts for both effects:

$$
\frac{D\mathbf{u}}{Dt} = \frac{\partial\mathbf{u}}{\partial t} + (\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{u}
$$

The first term is the local change in velocity at a fixed point. The second is the convective change experienced as the packet travels through space. The same idea applies to quantities carried by the fluid, such as dye, smoke density, or temperature.

## Forces Acting on the Fluid

The main forces in this model are pressure, viscosity, and external forces such as gravity.

Pressure is a scalar field. Its gradient describes how pressure changes through space, and the resulting force points from high pressure toward low pressure:

$$
\mathbf{F}_{pressure} = -V\nabla p
$$

Viscous forces represent internal friction between layers of fluid. They smooth out differences in velocity and are modeled with the Laplacian of the velocity field:

$$
\mathbf{F}_{viscosity} = \rho V \nu \nabla^2\mathbf{u}
$$

Here, $\nu$ is the kinematic viscosity. Finally, an external acceleration $\mathbf{f}$ contributes the force $\rho V\mathbf{f}$. Combining these forces with Newton's second law gives:

$$
\rho V \frac{D\mathbf{u}}{Dt} = -V\nabla p + \rho V\nu\nabla^2\mathbf{u} + \rho V\mathbf{f}
$$

After dividing by $\rho V$ and expanding the material derivative, we obtain the Navier-Stokes equation for an incompressible, Newtonian fluid:

$$
\frac{\partial\mathbf{u}}{\partial t} + (\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{u}
= -\frac{1}{\rho}\nabla p + \nu\nabla^2\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{f}
$$

## The Incompressibility Condition

The velocity field must also satisfy:

$$
\nabla\cdot\mathbf{u} = 0
$$

The divergence measures the net flow out of a point. A zero divergence means that fluid is not being created or destroyed: the amount entering a region equals the amount leaving it. This is a useful approximation for liquids and for many graphics simulations where changes in density are not important.

# Simulation Approaches

These equations are difficult to solve directly, especially for turbulent flows. Practical simulations therefore discretize the equations and solve an approximation at each time step. Most approaches fall into three categories:

1. **Eulerian methods** divide space into a grid and store fluid properties at fixed locations. Finite Difference, Finite Volume, and Finite Element methods are examples.
2. **Lagrangian methods** track particles as they move through space. Smoothed Particle Hydrodynamics (SPH) is a common example.
3. **Hybrid methods** combine both representations. Fluid-Implicit Particle (FLIP), for example, uses particles to represent the fluid while using a grid to enforce incompressibility.

For this project I chose an Eulerian solver. A grid makes it straightforward to apply finite differences, and it gives predictable memory and computation costs. Unity then provides a convenient way to visualize the result in real time.

# Coding the Simulation

## The Setup

I represent the simulation domain as a square grid. Density, pressure, and obstacle information are stored at cell centers. Velocity uses a staggered grid, also called a MAC (Marker-And-Cell) grid: horizontal velocity is stored on vertical cell faces and vertical velocity on horizontal faces.

![](/projects/FluidSim/velocity-grid.png)

Because face velocities sit between cells, each component has one extra entry in its corresponding direction. For an $N \times N$ grid:

```csharp:Scripts/FluidSim/FluidSolver.cs
public float[,] uGrid;
public float[,] vGrid;

uGrid = new float[gridSize + 1, gridSize];
vGrid = new float[gridSize, gridSize + 1];
```

This layout makes the divergence calculation local: the four face velocities surrounding a cell are exactly the values needed for that cell's net flow.

## Solving for Incompressibility

For a square cell with side length $\Delta x$, the divergence can be approximated with finite differences:

$$
\nabla\cdot\mathbf{u} \approx
\frac{u_{i+1,j}-u_{i,j}}{\Delta x}
+ \frac{v_{i,j+1}-v_{i,j}}{\Delta x}
$$

<Info>
  The indices increase upward and to the right. Thus, $u_{(i, j)}$ is the left
  face, $u_{(i + 1, j)}$ is the right face, $v_{(i, j)}$ is the bottom face, and
  $v_{(i, j + 1)}$ is the top face of cell $(i,j)$.
</Info>

The goal is to make this value zero for every fluid cell. Adjusting one face velocity changes the divergence of its neighboring cells too, so this is not a collection of independent fixes. It is a coupled system of equations.

### Lagrange Multipliers

Pressure provides the correction that selects a physically useful velocity field. A pressure gradient changes velocity according to:

$$
\mathbf{u}^{new} = \mathbf{u}^{old} - \frac{\Delta t}{\rho}\nabla p
$$

Substituting this correction into the incompressibility condition produces a Poisson equation for pressure. I solve it iteratively with a Jacobi-style relaxation step:

$$
p_{i,j} = \frac{1}{4}
\left(
p_{i+1,j}+p_{i-1,j}+p_{i,j+1}+p_{i,j-1}
- \rho\frac{\Delta x^2}{\Delta t}(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{u})_{i,j}
\right)
$$

After several iterations, I subtract the pressure gradient from the face velocities. More iterations reduce the remaining divergence, while fewer iterations are cheaper but produce a more compressible-looking result.

### Boundary Conditions

Obstacles need their own boundary conditions. For a solid wall, the normal component of velocity must be zero: fluid cannot pass through the wall. In the solver, faces adjacent to an obstacle are therefore assigned a zero normal velocity before pressure projection.

For a no-slip wall, tangential velocity is zero as well. A less restrictive free-slip boundary keeps tangential motion but still prevents fluid from crossing the obstacle. Choosing between these conditions changes how much the fluid appears to stick to the surface.

## Advecting the Fluid

Projection makes the velocity field divergence-free, but it does not move dye or smoke through the field. For a scalar quantity $q$, the advection equation is:

$$
\frac{\partial q}{\partial t}+\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla q=0
$$

I use semi-Lagrangian advection. For each cell, I trace a point backward through the velocity field:

$$
\mathbf{x}_{previous} = \mathbf{x} - \Delta t\,\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{x})
$$

The value at the current cell is then sampled from that previous position, using bilinear interpolation between neighboring cells. Tracing backward is stable even when the time step is relatively large, although it introduces numerical dissipation and gradually blurs sharp features.

The same backtracing idea can be used to sample a velocity field when calculating advection of velocity itself. I also apply viscosity as a diffusion step; this intentionally smooths the flow and helps keep the simulation stable.

## The Simulation Loop

Each frame advances the solver in a consistent order:

1. Apply external forces and user input to the face velocities.
2. Enforce obstacle boundary conditions.
3. Advect velocity through the current velocity field.
4. Diffuse velocity according to the viscosity.
5. Compute divergence and iteratively solve for pressure.
6. Subtract the pressure gradient to project the velocity field.
7. Advect density or dye using the projected velocity.
8. Upload the density field to a texture for rendering.

The order matters. In particular, pressure projection must happen before density is advected; otherwise the density would be transported by a field that still contains artificial sources and sinks.

## Rendering the Result

The simulation data is a grid, so it can be visualized as a texture. Each cell's density becomes a grayscale or color value, and a shader samples that texture across a quad. Higher density can represent more dye or smoke, while the velocity field controls how that pattern moves.

The video above shows vortex shedding: as the flow passes an obstacle, alternating vortices form behind it. The simulation is not intended to match a particular physical experiment exactly, but the result demonstrates how pressure, advection, viscosity, and boundaries combine to produce recognizable fluid behavior.

# Results and Limitations

The finished solver produces real-time two-dimensional effects with a small amount of code and a fixed memory cost. The MAC grid keeps the pressure projection well behaved, and semi-Lagrangian advection makes the simulation robust enough to run interactively.

There are still important trade-offs. A larger grid captures smaller vortices but costs more every frame. More pressure iterations improve incompressibility but reduce performance. Semi-Lagrangian advection is stable but dissipative, so energetic details fade over time. The solver is also two-dimensional and assumes constant density; it cannot reproduce every effect of a three-dimensional, compressible fluid.
